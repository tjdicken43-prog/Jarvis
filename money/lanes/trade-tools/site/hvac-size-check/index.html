Rough cooling size

What this is, and what it is not

The real answer is a Manual J load calculation (the ACCA method), which uses the actual walls, windows, ducts and local design temperatures. This page does a quick version: 25 BTU per square foot as a starting point for a mixed-humid climate like Arkansas, scaled by ceiling height, insulation, windows and sun, plus 400 BTU for each person past two. It then shows a range of 12% either way. That base figure is a rule of thumb we chose, not a standard, so treat the result as a ballpark.

One ton of cooling is 12,000 BTU per hour. The nominal sizes most equipment comes in are 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4 and 5 tons; the page shows the nearest.

Why not just size up?

An oversized air conditioner cools the air fast, shuts off, and never runs long enough to pull humidity out. The house feels clammy, the system starts and stops more, and comfort suffers. Right-sized and a little under is usually better than a size too big. Duct condition matters as much as tonnage.

Worked example

2,000 sq ft, 8 ft ceilings, average insulation, windows and sun, two people: 2,000 × 25 = 50,000 BTU, which is 4.2 tons. The range is 3.7 to 4.7 tons and the nearest nominal size is 4 tons. A Manual J on a tight, shaded house could land a full ton lower.

Questions

Can I replace a unit with the same tonnage as the old one?
Often, but not safely assumed. Many older systems were sized by rule of thumb and may be oversized. If the house was air sealed or re-insulated since, the load is lower.
Does this cover heating?
No. In much of the South cooling sets the size; in colder areas the heating load can. Run the heating side separately.
Is this good enough to quote from?
It is good enough to see whether a bid is far off. Quote from a measured load calculation.
